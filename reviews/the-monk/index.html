☆☆☆★★★★★★★: Matthew Lewis’s The Monk

Despite the fact that Lewis’s work has been unfairly villified by readers with substandard comprehension and critical thinking skills, one day when I finally regained access to my Penguin Classics library (it’s a long story and I don’t care to mention it), I decided it was time I read it.

As soon as I read the introduction, I realized the book and its author were presented in an overly negative light–perhaps unintentionally or even unconsciously. So I decided to give it a read.

And what a read it was. It is by far the most interesting English novel I have read so far. The art may be lacking a bit, but Lewis makes up for it in other ways: the book, despite its rather crude depictions of certain scenes, is actually one written for male audiences–and is meant to straighten them back to their occasionally-lost morality.

The bit where he seems to be criticizing the content of the Bible? The editor’s introduction made it appear as if Lewis was showing atheistic tendencies. This is a problem that I’ve found in many circles: proponents of certain ways of thinking usually read too much into some source and make it appear as one of their own (such as Shia scholars in Iran trying to present prominent Sufi masters as being Shi’ite, which is categorically untrue.).

But not Lewis. Any Muslim worth their salt knows that the Bible as it currently exists is not the real, original Holy Bible. For starters, the writing is substandard and weak, written by humans–not very educated or even eloquent ones at that. I suspect this is the reason many people leave Christianity or simply become atheists. I haven’t read the Bible seriously, but where I have occasionally chanced upon, it didn’t feel like a good investment of my time. Why would I be interested in a strange book written by various humans?

I don’t care that Claude (AI) whitewashes it as ‘God through people’. There’s no need for ’through people’; in Islam, The Most Exalted Almighty Creator has sent the Holy Book directly communicated to our Prophet (Peace and Blessings be upon him) through angel Gabriel (Alaihe Salam). The Holy Book should educate, elevate, and mesmerize not corrupt, confuse, or make one doubt its veracity. The book people call Bible nowadays is doing exactly the latter, and this is what Lewis was actually saying.

However, Lewis was also making very important religious points. Is someone chaste and good because they have shut themselves off from a world of temptation? The final point implied is that true chastity and piety occurs in spite of all the temptations. When you can sin and refuse to commit it, you are truly moral, as opposed to having no chance to commit such a sin yet.

I couldn’t put it down, and I wish he had written more books!
