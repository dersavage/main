☆☆☆★★★★★★★ Trollope: He knew he was right

TLDR in summary: Better than expected.

Pages: 800+

Finally! I started HKHWR in early 2019, but life happened and back it went into my library. In the meantime, seven years passed and I became a rather sophisticated reader. And since I don’t like loose ends, I decided to finish it, once and for all.

I say that phrase because back in 2019, I read all the way near the second half, but somehow I couldn’t go further. The shenanigans of the stupid cow Emily Rowley was too much for me, especially as at the time, I sort of depressed and busy, and the book, especially the first half was mostly about the quarell between husband and wife, and instead of elevating me and making me forget my woes, it was dampening my mood.

However, the willfulness and verbal judo of Emily Rowley against her pitiful, clueless, unwise, and cowardly husband was just a prelude to Trollope’s ambitious attack on radical feminism, and in that context, also on the importance of not creating evil fairies.

This comes to the fore in the second part when the rather strangely named Mr. Glascock tries to woo the American beauty, Caroline, but Caroline’s radical feminist ‘friend’, a woman who is portrayed in the most negative possible manner by Trollope, displays typical man-hating, anti-marriage actions and words; Trollope even calls her a rabid enthusiast, and she is the worst possible example of how a woman shouldn’t be according to Trollope’s conception of womanhood.

But he doesn’t stop there. Elsewhere he mocks the two miss Frenches, where one is an evil fairy, and the other a pitiful being who plays mind games (the very opposite of what Gestalt psychologists call the authentic person); Bella is unauthentic and mediocre, but Camilla is angry, dangerous and strong-willed.

Yet, starting from Camila and going to the other end, he shows a variety of woman archetypes, where on the other end, he shows a model woman, a truly beautiful being (Dorothy), but even there, Dorothy is not perfect and he shows the influence of environment on our innate core traits–how in the case of Dorothy, these environmental stimuli caused her to blossom, and discover her true, actualized self. In this, Trollope is well ahead of Albert Bandura.

There are other women whose archetypes fall between Dorothy and Bella, some of whom show potential troublesome tendencies (like Nora), though they are otherwise excellent in many respects.

The second half of the book, therefore, is less depressing and even hilarious in places, but the hilarity is of the Gogolian type: comi-tragic. Trollope takes care to attack inauthentic people every chance he gets, be they men or women (as he attacks Mr. Gibson), but the attacks are not vicious. He is mocking Gibson and people who think and behave like him.

All in all, it was great psychological novel, and it taught or reinforced many important psychological gems worth acquiring. It was not as exciting or as biting as Eustace Diamonds but certainly several orders of magnitude better than what Rider Haggard has written (I will attack Haggard in another review).
